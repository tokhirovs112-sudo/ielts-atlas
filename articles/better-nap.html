The Art of a Better Nap

Published November 4, 2026 · 6-minute read

There is a particular pleasure in closing your eyes in the middle of a busy day and waking up with the feeling that the world has become a little clearer. A well-timed nap can offer a welcome pause, helping some people feel more alert and ready to concentrate. Yet daytime sleep does not always leave us refreshed. Sometimes we wake up groggy; sometimes we lie there for ages without falling asleep; and sometimes an afternoon nap makes bedtime surprisingly difficult.

The difference often comes down to how and when we rest. A nap is not simply a shorter version of a full night's sleep. It works best when it fits around your usual sleep schedule and takes place in an environment that allows you to relax. These six ideas can help you make that small break more restorative.

1. Keep it short enough to wake up refreshed

For many adults, a brief nap of around 10 to 20 minutes is a useful starting point. It can provide a quick rest without giving the body as much opportunity to move into deeper stages of sleep. Waking from deeper sleep can sometimes cause temporary grogginess, often called sleep inertia.

Set an alarm before you settle down, and give yourself a few minutes after waking to become fully alert. If you regularly feel that a short nap is not enough, consider whether your nighttime sleep is sufficient rather than continually extending your daytime rest.

2. Choose the right moment

Nap timing matters. For many people, late morning or the early afternoon is less likely to interfere with nighttime sleep than a nap taken late in the day. Try to keep your nap at a fairly consistent time, especially if you find that an irregular schedule makes it harder to fall asleep at night.

Think of a nap as a small break in your day, not a replacement for a regular sleep routine. If you notice that daytime sleep is making it difficult to fall asleep at your usual bedtime, try moving it earlier or shortening it.

3. Give your eyes a break from the light

Bright surroundings can make it harder to settle down. If possible, choose a dim, quiet place and draw the curtains. You do not need to turn your room into a completely dark cave: the goal is simply to reduce distractions and make the space feel restful.

Put away bright screens before trying to sleep. If daylight still makes the room uncomfortable, a comfortable sleep mask may help, provided you can use it safely and easily.

4. Get comfortable, wherever you are

A familiar, comfortable place can make it easier to relax. If a bed or sofa is available, use it; if not, choose a safe place where you can sit back and support your head and neck. Loosen anything restrictive and make sure you will not be disturbed by an uncomfortable position.

Comfort is personal. Some people prefer lying down, while others find that reclining slightly is enough for a short rest. The best setup is one that helps you relax without leaving you stiff or uncomfortable when you wake.

5. Reduce sudden noise

You may not be able to control every sound around you, but you can often make the environment less disruptive. Close a door, silence notifications or let people nearby know that you are taking a short break. If background noise is unavoidable, gentle, steady sound may make sudden changes in volume less noticeable.

Earplugs can be an option for some people, as long as they are comfortable and you can still hear anything important. Avoid playing audio so loudly that it becomes another source of disturbance.

6. Create a calming pre-nap ritual

A simple routine can help signal that it is time to pause. You might lower the lights, put your phone aside, take a few slow breaths or listen to quiet background sounds. Some people enjoy a familiar, gentle scent, such as lavender, as part of a relaxing routine. Treat it as a personal preference rather than a guaranteed sleep remedy, and avoid fragrances that irritate you.

Repeating the same calming steps can make it easier to switch from work or study into rest. The ritual does not need to be elaborate; consistency is more useful than complexity.

A nap is a pause, not a cure-all

Daytime rest can be pleasant, but it cannot make up for a persistent lack of nighttime sleep. If you often feel extremely sleepy during the day, regularly need long naps, or remain tired despite allowing enough time for sleep, it is worth discussing the pattern with a healthcare professional. Ongoing sleep difficulties can have several causes, and a clinician can help you work out what is happening.

For an ordinary midday slump, though, a little planning can make a difference: keep the nap brief, choose an earlier time, make your surroundings comfortable and give yourself a moment to wake up properly. Rest does not have to take up half the afternoon to be worthwhile. Sometimes, a well-planned pause is exactly what the day needs.

This article offers general well-being information, not individual medical advice. Sleep needs and health circumstances differ from person to person.
